Neuronal Plasticity and Adult Learning

Elena Marchetti, David Okafor, Priya Raman

We examined how sustained study practice reshapes neural circuits in adults aged 25 to 60. Participants completed daily problem-solving tasks for eight weeks. Structural imaging showed increased cortical thickness in regions associated with working memory. These findings suggest that adult brains retain meaningful capacity for experience-dependent change.

Introduction

Most popular accounts treat the adult brain as largely fixed. Research over the past two decades has challenged that view by showing that experience continues to modify neural structure and function. This study asks whether deliberate cognitive practice produces measurable changes in adults across a wide age range.

Methods

We enrolled 84 participants, aged 25 to 60, with no prior diagnosis of neurological disease. Participants completed a 30-minute problem-solving task each weekday for eight weeks. Structural magnetic resonance imaging was collected before and after the study. We compared cortical thickness in regions involved in working memory, attention, and motor control.

Results

Participants who completed at least 80 percent of sessions showed a mean increase of 0.14 millimeters in prefrontal cortical thickness. The change was strongest in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and weakest in motor areas. Participants with higher baseline sleep quality showed slightly larger gains, though the difference did not reach conventional significance thresholds.

Discussion

These results support the view that adult brains remain responsive to structured cognitive effort. The effect sizes are modest, but they are consistent with prior studies linking practice to neural change. Future work should test whether shorter, more intense schedules produce similar effects and whether the changes persist after training stops.
